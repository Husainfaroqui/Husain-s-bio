Cell Membrane

The plasma membrane is a selectively permeable bilayer separating the cell's interior from its environment. It is composed of phospholipids, proteins, and cholesterol.

Each phospholipid has a hydrophilic (water-loving) head and two hydrophobic fatty acid tails. The bilayer arranges so heads face outward while tails form a hydrophobic core — a fluid mosaic model.

  • Phospholipid bilayer forms the structural backbone
  • Integral proteins span the full membrane thickness
  • Peripheral proteins attach to one surface only
  • Cholesterol stabilizes membrane fluidity in temperature changes
  • Selective permeability controls what enters and exits the cell
  • Glycoproteins serve as cell-identity and signaling markers

Mitochondria

Mitochondria are the energy-producing organelles of the cell, generating ATP through cellular respiration. They contain their own circular DNA, evidence of their ancient bacterial ancestry (endosymbiotic theory).

The inner membrane is extensively folded into cristae — dramatically increasing surface area for oxidative phosphorylation where ATP synthase molecules act as molecular motors to produce ATP.

  • Outer membrane: smooth, permeable to small molecules
  • Inner membrane: highly folded cristae, electron transport chain
  • Matrix: site of Krebs (TCA) cycle, contains mtDNA + ribosomes
  • ATP Synthase (F₁F₀): rotary molecular motor, produces ATP
  • Proton gradient drives ATP production (chemiosmosis)
  • ~36 ATP molecules produced per glucose molecule oxidized

Golgi Apparatus

The Golgi apparatus is the cell's "post office," receiving proteins from the endoplasmic reticulum, modifying them, sorting them, and shipping them to their final destinations inside or outside the cell.

It consists of flattened cisternae stacked in 4–8 layers. The cis face receives incoming ER vesicles; proteins are processed as they move through the stack; the trans face dispatches finished vesicles.

  • Receives unmodified proteins and lipids from rough ER
  • Adds and modifies carbohydrate chains (glycosylation)
  • Phosphorylates proteins and adds targeting signals
  • Sorts cargo by signal sequences for correct destination
  • Produces lysosomes containing digestive enzymes
  • Critical for secretion of hormones, enzymes, and antibodies

How the Heart Works

The heart is a muscular pump beating ~100,000 times daily, moving blood through the entire body in a continuous closed circuit of two interconnected loops.

Four chambers — two atria (receiving) and two ventricles (pumping) — work in a precise coordinated rhythm called the cardiac cycle. The right side handles pulmonary circulation; the left side drives systemic circulation.

How Neurons Work

Neurons are the fundamental units of the nervous system — electrically excitable cells that transmit information across the body at remarkable speed through electrical and chemical signals.

The human brain contains ~86 billion neurons, each forming up to 10,000 synaptic connections. Myelinated axons can transmit signals at up to 120 m/s through saltatory conduction — jumping between nodes of Ranvier.

The Human Brain

The human brain — ~1.5 kg of densely interconnected neurons — is the most complex structure in the known universe, governing thought, memory, emotion, movement, and every bodily function simultaneously.

The cerebral cortex is divided into four lobes with specialized functions. The cerebellum coordinates fine motor control; the brain stem governs automatic vital functions. Connectivity between regions enables consciousness itself.
